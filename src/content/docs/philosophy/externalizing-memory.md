*A seed page — the first thing in this section, meant to be argued with and
rewritten.*

There's a common objection to writing things down: the effort to record a thing
often exceeds the effort to just look it up again. For a single lookup, that's
true. It's the wrong comparison.

## The real cost isn't the lookup

When you re-derive something for the third time, you don't just pay the search
cost. You pay the cost of not trusting your own past conclusions — of having to
re-establish that the thing works, re-check the version, re-confirm the flag
didn't change. Re-deriving is not a cheap read; it's a full re-verification, and
it's why the fourth time feels as slow as the first.

Writing it down converts that from an open question into a settled one. Not
because the note is authoritative, but because it's *yours* — it records what
worked on your machine, with your setup, at a known time.

## Compression as understanding

The useful side effect is that writing forces compression, and compression is
where understanding actually happens.

You can follow a tutorial to a working result without understanding any of it.
The gap only becomes visible when you try to write the short version. Which of
those twelve steps mattered? Which was incidental to that author's setup? Which
one was cargo-culted from a decade-old forum post? Answering those is
understanding, and there's no way to fake it in a document you have to use later.

This is the honest argument for keeping notes even when you'll never reread
them. The document is a byproduct. The rewrite is the work.

## Where it goes wrong

Two failure modes, both common enough to plan around.

**The write-only archive.** Notes accumulate, nothing is ever found, and the
system becomes an elaborate way of forgetting things carefully. The fix is
structural: findability has to be cheaper than re-deriving, or the archive is
decorative. Search that works, one obvious location per topic, and honest titles
do more than any tagging scheme.

**The stale runbook.** Worse than no note, because it carries authority it hasn't
earned. A command that worked in 2023 and silently fails now costs more than a
blank page — you'll trust it, then debug the wrong layer for an hour. This is
what dates and "last updated" stamps are for: not provenance, but a warning
label.

Both failures come from treating the collection as an asset that only grows.
It isn't. It needs pruning, and deleting a page you no longer trust is
maintenance, not loss.

## The test

A knowledge base is working if, when you hit a familiar problem, your first
instinct is to check your own notes rather than a search engine — and that
instinct is usually rewarded.

If it isn't rewarded, the problem is rarely motivation. It's that the notes are
in the wrong shape: too long to scan, too vague to act on, or filed somewhere
you'd never think to look. All three are fixable, and all three are more worth
fixing than adding another page.
